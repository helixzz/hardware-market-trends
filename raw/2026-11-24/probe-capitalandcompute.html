Server RAM prices: what a DDR5 RDIMM costs in 2026

Tracker· Updated November 16, 2026

Server memory is where the shortage started and where the money is. It is also the channel with two prices: the street price of a new registered ECC module, and the contract price a cloud provider pays, which is capped for some buyers and not for others. This page tracks both, and the gap between them explains why enterprise budgets broke before consumer ones did.

How much does server RAM cost per GB in 2026?

A new DDR5 ECC RDIMM costs about $29.37 to $43.69 per gigabyte at retail as of November 16, 2026, against a tracked median of $42.29. That is roughly 2.2x the $19.37 per GB a consumer DDR5 kit averages. In module terms, a 64GB RDIMM runs about $2,561 and a 128GB module about $4,990. On the contract side TrendForce expects conventional DRAM contract prices, which cover server DRAM, to rise 10 to 15% quarter-over-quarter in 4Q26, but several US cloud providers hold multi-year agreements that bar suppliers from raising their prices, so that increase lands disproportionately on buyers without one.

The channel gap: same gigabyte, two prices

At the capacities both channels sell, a gigabyte of registered ECC memory costs more than twice what the same gigabyte costs in a consumer kit. Part of that is real extra silicon, since a registered module carries a buffer and ECC dies. The rest is who is bidding: the consumer channel is picking through what a shortage leaves behind, while the server channel is the reason the shortage exists.

Why buyers are downshifting module density

Per gigabyte, this channel has no clean density curve: the six tracked capacities run from $29.37 (256GB) to $43.69 (16GB), and the order reshuffles between reads as single listings come and go. TrendForce has observed a density downshift independently. In its words: CSPs and OEMs have gradually adjusted their RDIMM configurations, with some systems shifting from 96GB and 128GB modules to 32GB and 64GB modules. By 4Q26 suppliers had followed: TrendForce reports they have increased the share of lower-capacity RDIMMs in their product mix to match that demand. At tracked street prices the downshift does not pay this time: two 64GB modules cost about $5,122 against $4,990 for one 128GB module, $132 more, and use an extra DIMM slot. The buyers TrendForce describes pay contract prices, which are not public, so the retail listings cannot confirm the saving they are chasing.

The contract market, and who is shielded from it

Everything above is a street price for a new module. The prices that actually move the AI build-out are negotiated contracts, and TrendForce publishes those as quarter-over-quarter changes rather than levels. For 4Q26 itpublished no separate server figure and expects conventional DRAM, which covers server DRAM and which it still calls undersupplied on the server side, up 10 to 15%, and NAND flash up 15 to 20%. The distribution of that increase is the part worth reading twice. Several U.S.-based CSPs have entered into multi-year long-term agreements which, in TrendForce's words, restrict suppliers from raising prices for these clients. From 3Q26 the primary source of server DRAM price increases shifted toward customers without an LTA, and toward incremental supply sold outside an LTA to existing LTA customers. For 4Q26 TrendForce still expects increases for some suppliers to lag the market average because of pricing mechanisms set in those agreements.

Consumer price tolerance is the brake. TrendForce reported in July that consumer customers had reached their limit, which moderated the 3Q26 increase to 13-18%, and for 4Q26 it expects consumer DRAM to keep rising at a slower pace while notebook inventories reflecting higher component costs weigh on end-market sales. AI server demand is what keeps the conventional DRAM forecast at 10-15%. That is a demand-side ceiling, not a supply-side fix: the moderation comes from consumer buyers refusing to pay, while cloud providers keep buying ahead of an expected 2027 squeeze. For the signals that would show a genuine turn, see whether the 2026 memory cycle is peaking.

Server RAM prices: frequently asked questions

How much does server RAM cost per GB in 2026?
About $29.37 to $43.69 per gigabyte for a new in-stock DDR5 ECC RDIMM, with a tracked median of $42.29 as of November 16, 2026. These are single-retailer street prices for new modules, not the contract prices hyperscalers negotiate, and not the much cheaper used-server-pull market. A consumer DDR5 kit averages $19.37 per GB over the same week, so registered ECC memory carries roughly a 2.2x premium per gigabyte.
Why is server memory more expensive per GB than desktop memory?
Three reasons compound. Registered ECC modules carry extra silicon (a register or buffer plus the ECC dies), so there is more part per gigabyte. They ship in far higher capacities per module, and capacity is exactly what repriced in this shortage. And they sit in the demand path of the AI build-out, which is the buyer with the least price sensitivity in the market. The consumer channel gets the leftovers of a shortage; the server channel is the shortage.
Are cloud providers paying these price increases?
Not all of them, and that is the most underreported mechanic in this market. Several U.S.-based CSPs have entered into multi-year long-term agreements which, in TrendForce's words, restrict suppliers from raising prices for these clients. From 3Q26 the primary source of server DRAM price increases shifted toward customers without an LTA, and toward incremental supply sold outside an LTA to existing LTA customers. For 4Q26 TrendForce still expects increases for some suppliers to lag the market average because of pricing mechanisms set in those agreements. In practice a large US cloud provider with an agreement signed before the squeeze holds its price while a smaller buyer, an enterprise refresh or a regional host pays the full 10 to 15% quarterly increase. Reported "server memory prices" are therefore an average of two very different experiences.
Why are buyers switching to smaller RDIMM modules?
TrendForce reports the shift from the buyer side: CSPs and OEMs have gradually adjusted their RDIMM configurations, with some systems shifting from 96GB and 128GB modules to 32GB and 64GB modules. By 4Q26 suppliers had followed: TrendForce reports they have increased the share of lower-capacity RDIMMs in their product mix to match that demand. The retail listings tracked here do not show the saving this time: two 64GB modules cost about $5,122 against $4,990 for one 128GB module, so the motive sits in contract prices and supply, which are not public. Several capacities rest on one or two tracked listings, so treat them as thin-liquidity readings rather than a settled market price.
When will server RAM prices stop rising?
No forecaster on record has called a peak in the contract market. What has changed is the pace: TrendForce now expects the 4Q26 increase to moderate to 10 to 15% for conventional DRAM, partly because consumer price tolerance is the brake. TrendForce reported in July that consumer customers had reached their limit, which moderated the 3Q26 increase to 13-18%, and for 4Q26 it expects consumer DRAM to keep rising at a slower pace while notebook inventories reflecting higher component costs weigh on end-market sales. AI server demand is what keeps the conventional DRAM forecast at 10-15%. Note the asymmetry in that sentence: the moderation comes from consumer buyers refusing, not from supply arriving. AI server demand is still the bid, and buyers are stockpiling against an even tighter 2027.
